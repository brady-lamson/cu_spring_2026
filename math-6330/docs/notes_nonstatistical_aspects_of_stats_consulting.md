# Abstract

This paper creates a model for handling the non stats aspects of stats consulting. It breaks things down into 4 key parts.

1. Identification of relevant aspects of the problem situation
2. Definition of the clients goals
3. Determination of the actions to be taken
4. Discussion of various aspects of the consulting relationship and who will do what when. 

# Intro

A key assumption to this model is that it is *problem minded* not *solution minded*. The philosophy here is that statistical consultant's most important role is helping the client understand the nature of their problems better. This can help a client be more independent by the end of the process. Two key goals arise from this for the statistician.

1. Solve an immediately statistical or methodological problem.
2. Increase the capacity of clients to understand and solve similar problems when they arise in the future. 

This approach requires the statistican to have adequate training to actually be good at their job. A deep understanding of the scientific method cannot be replaced with good consulting skills. 

# A model for statistical consulting

## The first meeting

This section emphasizes the obvious importance of the first meeting. Goes without saying. Returning to parts 1 and 2 of the consulting model, these are CRUCIAL because they help the consultant avoiding finding the right answer to the wrong problem. Always keep focused on their needs and their goals. 

## Identification of the situaton

Some common questions a consultant must understand about the problem:

a. Is the client under time constraints
b. Are there other collaborators?
c. Does the client have complete autonomy over the data?
d. Has similar research been done elsewhere?
e. What attempts have been made to solve the current problem?
f. and so on from there.

My takeaway here is that it's crucial we get on the same level as the client. Where are they at? What is the problem really.

The problem MUST be understood before asking any statistical questions. This is similar to doing EDA right? You need to know what columns actually represent.

> Bliss (1969) notes that his "first rule would be to avoid answering any questions on statistics asked initially by the client" UNTIL the background of the problem is ascertained. 

This is because the statistical solution proposed by the client is likely not the one most pertinent to their problem. 

Both clients and consultants have assumptions about what information will be important/relevant. This can manifest in a few ways. One example is a client restricting the information they present to the consultant to just they stuff they think is important. Some clients will show the consultant EVERYTHING and drown them in all of the details. 

Because this is all such a mess, the consultant must FREQUENTLY reflect back to the client their interpretation of the situation. This is because the consultant will likely be off base multiple times and this is crucial for getting an accurate course at the start. 

The client may ask questions like "Shouldn't I perform MANOVA on this data set" and, even if the question IS relevant, to hold off on answering that until the full problem scope is understood. Learn to say **"I'm sorry, but I don't have enough information about what you are trying to do to give a thoughtful answer. Why don't we discuss the problem in more detail or set up an appointment to explore that idea"**

Also if you are not a subject matter expert on a topic for the problem, the client NEEDS TO KNOW THIS.
